# Draft response to Joe — Blueprint v1.0 feedback

Subject: Your blueprint is 70% right — three changes before we build anything

Joe,

First, the good news: this is a strong plan, and most of it survives contact with the market research we just finished. The things you got right are the things most people get wrong — don't buy trucks, sign up operators before chasing customers, keep the first version small, prove the product with real campaigns before launching wide, and hold everyone to written approval gates. All of that stays.

We also finished a deep market study (full report attached — every claim sourced). It changes three big things about the build, and I want to walk you through them in plain terms.

**1. Bidding won't work — we need listed prices and instant booking instead.**

The blueprint is built around customers posting a request and operators competing with quotes. Here's the problem: there are only about 85–90 billboard trucks in all of Georgia and Florida combined, and most companies own one to four of them. For any given neighborhood and date, there will usually be one, maybe two, operators who can even do the job. You can't run an auction with one bidder. And these are phone-and-email businesses — if we wait for them to log in and type up quotes, we've rebuilt the exact "wait for callbacks" problem your page 4 complains about.

The good news is we don't need bidding. Prices in this industry are already standardized — three different national brokers publish nearly identical rates ($800–$2,400 a day for vinyl trucks, $1,200–$3,600 for LED). So the customer experience should be: pick a date, an area, and a truck type, see the price, book it, pay. Quotes become the exception for big or unusual jobs, not the default. This actually makes the software simpler and cheaper to build, not harder.

**2. Let's make money in month one, not month four.**

The three-month plan is: design, then build, then pilot. Revenue only shows up at the very end, after we've built dashboards for three different kinds of users. Instead: put up the simple customer-facing site and booking form in the first few weeks, and have our own team (with our AI agents doing the heavy lifting) play the role of the operator dashboard behind the scenes — calling operators, confirming trucks, collecting proof. We take a 15–20% cut on real campaigns from week three or four onward.

Every one of those early campaigns pays us three times: it's revenue, it's proof for investors, and it tells us exactly what to automate next. The operator dashboard gets built after we've run 25–50 campaigns and know what operators actually need — which is exactly the "prove the transaction before expanding" philosophy from your own executive summary, just aimed at transactions instead of software.

**3. There's a legal step missing, and it has to come first.**

The blueprint never mentions city sign laws — and this industry lives and dies by them. Our research found: Key West bans billboard trucks outright, Miami Beach effectively does too (we already planned around both), Jacksonville's city code contains language that's openly hostile to mobile billboards, and Atlanta, Orlando, Tampa, and Savannah all have sign rules we couldn't fully confirm from public records. The one place we *confirmed* is clearly workable for moving trucks is mainland Miami.

So before anyone writes code, we spend about a week with a Georgia and a Florida land-use attorney getting a definitive answer for all six cities. That answer decides our launch map. And by the way — your document assumes Miami throughout ("a truck in Brickell," "recruit operators in Miami"), while our original plan said Atlanta first. The research actually sides with you: Miami has the clearest legal footing and the most trucks. But we need to make that call explicitly, together, not have two documents assuming different cities.

**A few smaller things the blueprint should absorb:**

- **We have competitors, and the plan should name them.** AdQuick is the big online player, but their self-serve product starts at $25,000 — which is precisely why our under-$25K lane is open. Blip proved small businesses will buy outdoor ads online, but they only do fixed billboards.
- **Google ads will be more expensive than the plan assumes.** The phone-based brokers (Blue Line Media and others) already own the search results for "mobile billboard Miami." We shouldn't out-spend them there on day one — our cheapest customers are the ones who *can't* phone around: political campaigns (2026 is a record election year in both our states) and out-of-town convention exhibitors booking trucks around the Orlando and Miami convention centers.
- **Put numbers on success.** The pilot shouldn't just prove "a transaction completed." It needs to prove two economic facts: operators accept our 15% cut, and customers come back and book again. If either fails, the model fails, and we want to know in month two, not month twelve.

**The ask:** your own Section 6 says to interview 5 operators and 10 advertisers before finalizing the design — let's run those interviews *first*, before we commit to the quote-bidding screens in Sections 9–12. I'd bet the interviews tell us the same thing the research did. Happy to walk through the full report whenever you want.

— [Founder]
